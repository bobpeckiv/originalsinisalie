Verses / The Prodigal Son

The parable

The Prodigal Son

Luke 15:11–32

In Gary Renard's The Disappearance of the Universe, his teachers tell him the most important story in the whole Bible is the parable of the prodigal son. If you threw out everything else and kept that one, it would be enough. I think they're right.

The story

Lost and found

A younger son asks his father for his inheritance early, which many readers hear as close to saying "I wish you were dead." He takes it to a far country and blows all of it. Then a famine hits. He ends up feeding pigs, so hungry he wants to eat their food. For a Jewish audience, you can't sink lower than that.

But when he came to himself he said, 'How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough to spare, and I'm dying with hunger!'

Luke 15:17

He rehearses his speech: I'm not worthy to be your son, make me a hired hand. And he heads home.

But while he was still far off, his father saw him, and was moved with compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.

Luke 15:20

The father doesn't wait for the apology. He runs. He cuts the speech off and calls for the best robe, a ring, sandals, and the fattened calf. "For this, my son, was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found."

What it means

It was never about what he did

The son's mistakes don't decide how the father reacts. Who he is decides it. He's his son, and nothing he did in the far country could change that. The son is us. The father is the Creator. We wander off into a world of things that never satisfy, and we decide we've ruined it, that we can't go back, that we aren't worthy. And the whole time the father is watching the road.

One small detail I love: when the servant explains the party to the older brother, he says the father is celebrating because he got his son back "safe and healthy" (Luke 15:27). That's what coming home looks like. Not punished. Made well.

The other brother

There's one in all of us

The parable doesn't end at the party. The older son, the one who stayed and did everything right, comes in from the field, hears the music, and won't go inside. All these years I served you, and you never threw me a party.

Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours.

Luke 15:31

That's the do-gooder in all of us, the part that keeps score and thinks the lost deserve less. Jesus leaves the story open. We never find out whether the older brother goes in. Both sons are loved the same, and only one of them knows it yet. Pretty good storyteller.

It's also the third story in a row. Luke 15 starts with a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to find one, then a woman who turns her house upside down for one lost coin. Each one ends in a party.

Across traditions

The same story in the Lotus Sutra

There's a near twin in Buddhism. In the fourth chapter of the Lotus Sutra, a son runs away from his wealthy father and wanders for years in poverty. The father never stops looking. When the son finally drifts back, he doesn't recognize his father and is terrified of the rich man's house. So the father disguises himself, hires the son for the lowest work, and slowly raises him up over many years. On his deathbed he gathers everyone and announces: this is my son, and everything I have is his.

Christianity says we think we're sinners. Buddhism says we think we're ignorant. Hinduism and A Course in Miracles say we think we're separate. Same story. We're lost children who were never really lost, waiting to remember our inheritance.
