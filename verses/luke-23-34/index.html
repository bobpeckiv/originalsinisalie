Verses / Luke 23:34

The verse

"Father, forgive them"

Luke 23:34

Hardly anyone is doing this two thousand years later. Love your enemies isn't a nice idea Jesus mentioned once. It's the most radical thing he taught, and on the cross he did it.

The teaching

Love your enemies

You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor, and hate your enemy.' But I tell you, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you, that you may be children of your Father who is in heaven.

Matthew 5:43–45

That's from the Sermon on the Mount. Then, at the end of Luke's Gospel, he lives it. Arrested on charges that made no sense, beaten, paraded through the city, nailed up in front of his mother and his friends, he prays for the men doing it:

Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing.

Luke 23:34

It's one thing to teach that on a hillside. It's almost unbelievable to pray it while it's happening to you. This is the reason we're still talking about a working-class carpenter from a small town in Galilee two thousand years later. (Some early manuscripts leave the line out. Here's why many scholars still think it belongs.)

Luke's sequel, the Book of Acts, shows it caught on. As Stephen, the first Christian martyr, is being stoned to death, he kneels and cries out, "Lord, don't hold this sin against them!" (Acts 7:60).

Living it

A piano player and the Klan

In 1983 a Black piano player named Daryl Davis was playing country and boogie-woogie in a bar in a small Maryland town, to a room full of white people. Afterward a man came up and said he'd never heard a Black man play like Jerry Lee Lewis. Davis told him Jerry Lee had learned from Black blues and boogie-woogie players, and that he was a friend of his. They got a drink. Over that drink the man told him he was in the Ku Klux Klan.

They became friends. Over the following decades Davis befriended dozens of Klansmen, sat with them, listened to them, and kept showing up. Many of them eventually quit the Klan and handed him their robes. His point is simple: when two enemies are talking, they aren't fighting.

He had every right to hate them. He chose to see the human being instead. That's praying for those who persecute you, in a bar, with a drink in your hand.

Living it

Befriending a Nazi

My grandfather, Tommy Rochelle, was a radio DJ before World War II, so the Army put him in communications near the front. One cold night in Germany he walked out of his tent and came face to face with a German soldier. Both of them froze, scared, each looking at the enemy.

Once they realized it was just the two of them, things cooled down. The German had clearly been outside a long time. So my grandfather invited him in for coffee and a smoke, and gave him an empty bunk. The next morning he radioed his commander, proud as can be: "I have captured a Nazi, sir!" "How many?" "One."

The German stayed a few days, warm and fed, and before he left they traded keepsakes. My mother still has the knife he gave my grandfather. It's one of our family's most cherished things, not because of what it stood for, but because for a few nights two men in a war were just two men. If you can befriend a Nazi, you can befriend anyone.

Why it works

Meet them where they are

My wife's uncle spent decades as an interrogator for U.S. Customs and Border Protection, the guy they'd call at 2 a.m. when they caught somebody high up in a cartel. I asked him once what young officers get wrong. "They're too tough," he said. They walk in angry, trying to be action heroes, and all it does is pump more fear into the room. He got the truth because he saw the human being under the armor.

If a tough old interrogator says toughness is the mistake, it might be worth sitting with.
