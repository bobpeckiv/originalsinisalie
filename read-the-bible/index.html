A guide

How to read the Bible again

For anyone who grew up with it, left it, or never opened it

People ask me this all the time, usually right after someone hands them a King James. You don't have to read the Bible as an infallible rulebook or throw it out. You can read it like an archaeologist and a mystic at the same time: dig into the history, then listen for the teaching underneath. Read that way, the Bible comes alive.

Where to start

Don't start at the beginning

Front to back is the hardest way in. Genesis to Leviticus can lose you before you meet Jesus at all. And plenty of people get told to start with John, which is backwards: John was the last gospel written. Here's the order I'd suggest:

  1. Mark

    The earliest gospel, written around 70 CE, and the shortest. You can read it in an afternoon. It's the closest we have to the plain story.

  2. The Sermon on the Mount

    Matthew chapters 5 through 7. The Beatitudes, love your enemies, the Lord's Prayer, don't judge. If Jesus had a manifesto, this is it.

  3. Luke

    The gospel of the outsider and the poor, with the Good Samaritan (chapter 10) and the Prodigal Son (chapter 15), two of the most radical stories ever told.

  4. The Gospel of Thomas

    114 sayings of Jesus with no story wrapped around them, found in Egypt in 1945. Some scholars date its core as early as the gospels in the Bible. Read it next to them and watch how many sayings you recognize.

  5. John, last

    Once you know the earlier gospels, John's poetry makes sense as poetry. It's the spiritual gospel, full of "I am" statements meant for the inner circle. (More on that here.)

  6. Then Paul, carefully

    Thirteen letters carry Paul's name, but scholars broadly agree he wrote seven: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon. Some of the harshest lines people quote at you come from the other six.

Which Bible

Pick a translation

What I read

NRSV (and its update, the NRSVue). It's the translation scholars use, and it tries hardest to say what the old manuscripts say.

What churches use

NIV is the contemporary church standard. Readable, but its translators made theological choices you'll start to notice.

The King James is gorgeous English, but it's 1611 English, translated before many of the oldest manuscripts we have today were discovered. Read it for the poetry. No translation is perfect, and that's the point: every Bible you've ever held is somebody's interpretation.

How to read any passage

Four questions

  • Who's he talking to? Crowds got plain teachings, smaller groups got parables, and the disciples got the mystical stuff (Mark 4:10–11).
  • When was this written, and for whom? Matthew was written for a Jewish community reeling after Rome destroyed the Temple. That context explains a lot of its anger.
  • Is it a symbol? When Jesus curses a fig tree for having no figs out of season (Mark 11), it's not a tantrum. The tree stands for the Temple, which had fallen by the time Mark wrote.
  • Does it sound like love? Jesus said the whole law hangs on loving God and loving your neighbor (Matthew 22:40). If a reading makes you more afraid and less loving, keep digging.

And yes, it's okay to pick and choose. Everyone does, including the people who say they don't. The question is whether you choose from fear or from love.
