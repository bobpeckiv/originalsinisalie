A guide

The mystics of every tradition

From the outer to the inner

Every religion has an outside and an inside. The mystics are the inside. They're the people in every tradition who weren't satisfied hearing about God secondhand and went looking for themselves. What's strange is how much they sound like each other, across centuries and continents, people who never could have read each other's books.

Christianity

The Christian mystics

You don't have to leave Christianity to find this. Meister Eckhart, a German Dominican friar in the 1300s, preached that "the eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me." The anonymous English author of The Cloud of Unknowing wrote that God can be reached by love but never by thought. Julian of Norwich, an anchoress who lived walled into a cell beside a church, heard Christ tell her that "all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well." John of the Cross named the dark night of the soul. Teresa of Ávila mapped the soul as an interior castle with God in the innermost room.

Many of them were investigated, censored, or condemned by their own church. Eckhart was tried for heresy at the end of his life. That's a pattern you'll see in every tradition below. (More on Eckhart and the single eye.)

Early Christianity

The Gnostics

In the first centuries after Jesus there were many kinds of Christians, and some of them, later called Gnostics, put the weight on gnosis: not head knowledge but direct, inner knowing. Their writings were condemned and mostly lost, until 1945, when farmers digging near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt found a buried jar of ancient books, among them the Gospel of Thomas.

They weren't perfect. Some Gnostic groups looked down on ordinary believers, which is its own kind of spiritual pride. But some of them chose who would lead each service by drawing lots, because anyone could be an instrument of the divine. (More on Thomas.)

Islam

The Sufis

Sufism isn't a separate sect. It's the mystical heart that runs through Sunni and Shia Islam alike, the path of the heart and of love for the Beloved. The name likely comes from suf, the rough wool early ascetics wore. Sufis gave the world Rumi and Hafez, and the whirling dervishes who turn and turn in prayer.

Rabia of Basra, an eighth-century woman, was said to run through the streets with a torch in one hand and a bucket of water in the other: the torch to burn down paradise, the water to put out the fires of hell, so that people would love God for God alone. The goal Sufis describe is fana, the passing away of the separate self in God. When the mystic al-Hallaj cried out "I am the Truth," the authorities in Baghdad had him executed in 922.

Judaism

Kabbalah

Kabbalah means "that which is received." Its great book, the Zohar, the Book of Radiance, surfaced in 13th-century Spain as a mystical commentary on the Torah, traditionally credited to a second-century rabbi. It calls the deepest reality of God Ein Sof, "without end," beyond any name or form, known to us only through ten emanations, the sefirot.

Jewish tradition also teaches four levels of reading scripture: the plain meaning, the hint, the interpretation, and the secret, sod. It's the same move every tradition here makes. There's a surface reading, and there's a deeper one waiting underneath.

China

Taoism

The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name.

Tao Te Ching, chapter 1 (James Legge, 1891)

The Tao, the Way, is as big an idea as God: the source and substance of everything, including us, and too real to be put into words. The Tao Te Ching is credited to the sage Laozi. Taoist writing loves paradox. Softness beats hardness. Emptiness is useful. Doing nothing gets things done.

Its other great teacher, Zhuangzi, once dreamed he was a butterfly, happily fluttering around. When he woke up, he couldn't tell whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it was Zhuangzi.

Buddhism

Zen

Zen tells its origin like this. One day the Buddha sat before his monks and, instead of teaching, held up a single flower. No one understood except one monk, Mahakashyapa, who smiled. That smile was the whole teaching, passed on without words.

Zen came to China with the semi-legendary monk Bodhidharma, picked up a lot of Taoist flavor along the way, and went on to Japan. It's less interested in belief than in waking up right here. A monk once asked Master Zhaozhou to teach him. "Have you eaten your rice porridge?" "Yes." "Then wash your bowl." My toddler taught me more Zen than any book. (More on that.)

Hinduism

Advaita Vedanta

Advaita means "not two." It's the teaching that your deepest self, atman, and the formless absolute, Brahman, were never separate. The Upanishads put it in three words: tat tvam asi, "you are that." The philosopher Shankara systematized it in the 8th century, and in the 20th century Ramana Maharshi and Nisargadatta Maharaj made it plain again for anyone who would listen.

Ramana's whole practice fits in one question: Who am I? Not the body, not the thoughts, not the story. Keep asking, and what's left is the "I am" that was here before any of it. (More on "I am.")

What they share

Theologians may quarrel

There's a line, often attributed to Eckhart, that the mystics of the world speak the same language. Whoever said it first, it holds up. Across all of these traditions you keep hearing the same few things: that God or the Real is found within, that the separate self is the obstacle, that love matters more than doctrine, that the institution can't contain the experience, and that the truth is closer than your own breath.

Mysticism, in the end, is just turning inward while everyone and everything else is pointing outward. (Are all religions true?)
