A guide

The Gospels disagree

And that's freeing, not frightening

My brother-in-law, a devout Catholic I love dearly, once listened to me go on about the mystics for an entire evening. Then he said, kindly, "I appreciate what you're saying, but I just don't think it's okay to pick and choose." I hear that a lot. But the Gospels themselves don't agree on the details. Once you see that, picking and choosing isn't a betrayal of the Bible. It's the only honest way to read it.

How we got four

Four authors, four communities, four decades

  • Jesus taught in Aramaic, around 30 CE.
  • Decades of oral tradition followed, stories told and retold.
  • Mark was written first, in Greek, around 70 CE, right as Rome destroyed the Temple.
  • Matthew and Luke came next, around 80 to 95 CE. Both used Mark, plus a lost collection of sayings scholars call Q.
  • John came last, around 90 to 110 CE, and is so different that by 200 CE Clement of Alexandria was calling it the "spiritual gospel."

Then came centuries of hand copying. We have over five thousand Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, and no two are exactly alike. Most of the differences are spelling slips, but not all of them.

Places they don't line up

  1. Two different family trees.

    Matthew traces Jesus back to Abraham through King Solomon, and names Joseph's father Jacob (Matthew 1:1–16). Luke goes all the way back to Adam through a different son of David, Nathan, and names Joseph's father Heli (Luke 3:23–38).

  2. The temple, at opposite ends.

    In Mark, Jesus overturns the money changers' tables in his final week, and it helps get him killed (Mark 11:15–18). In John, it happens at the very start of his ministry (John 2:13–16).

  3. A sign, or no sign?

    In Mark, Jesus says "no sign will be given to this generation" (Mark 8:12). In Matthew, no sign will be given "but the sign of Jonah the prophet" (Matthew 12:39).

  4. Who was at the tomb?

    Mark: a young man in white (16:5). Matthew: an angel who rolled the stone away (28:2). Luke: two men in dazzling clothes (24:4). John: Mary Magdalene finds it empty, and later sees two angels (20:1, 20:12).

  5. His last words.

    A psalm of abandonment in Mark and Matthew, forgiveness and peace in Luke, and "It is finished" in John. (More here.)

  6. Mark's ending.

    The oldest manuscripts of Mark stop at 16:8, with the women running from the empty tomb, afraid. The appearances, the snake handling, and the ascension in 16:9–20 were added later.

So now what

Get archaeological

None of this means the Gospels are worthless. The Sermon on the Mount is sublime. The Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son can still crack your heart open. It means the Bible is a human document, written by real people for real communities, with all the fingerprints that leaves. Divinely inspired? From where I sit, absolutely, the way all great scripture is. Infallible? The texts themselves say no.

So dig. Ask who wrote each passage, when, and for whom. Keep what carries the teaching of love. Set down what was added to scare people into line. Everyone picks and chooses, including the people who say they don't. The question is whether you choose from fear or from love.
