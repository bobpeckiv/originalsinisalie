The humor of Christ

He was funnier than church made him sound

We picture Jesus as solemn, all stained glass and serious eyes. But listen to the images he actually uses: a man with a lumber beam stuck in his eye trying to perform eye surgery on his friend, a camel squeezing through a needle, a guy swallowing a whole camel. That's comedy. The Quaker writer Elton Trueblood wrote a whole book on it in 1964, The Humor of Christ, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

The plank

The carpenter's joke

Why do you see the speck that is in your brother's eye, but don't consider the beam that is in your own eye? Or how will you tell your brother, 'Let me remove the speck from your eye;' and behold, the beam is in your own eye? You hypocrite! First remove the beam out of your own eye, and then you can see clearly to remove the speck out of your brother's eye.

Matthew 7:3–5

Jesus was a tektōn, a builder, a guy who worked with wood. So picture it the way his crowd would have: a man with a roof beam sticking out of his face, leaning in close, squinting, offering to help you with a little speck of sawdust. It's slapstick. And it's still the most accurate description of judgment ever given.

The divider

"Who made me a divider over you?"

And one of the company said unto him, Master, speak to my brother, that he divide the inheritance with me. And he said unto him, Man, who made me a judge or a divider over you?

Luke 12:13–14 (KJV)

A man shows up mid-sermon with a family money fight and tries to deputize Jesus as his probate lawyer. Jesus' answer has perfect comic timing: Man. Who made me the divider here?

And it's more than a dodge. The one who sees no division refuses to become the divider. He turns right to the crowd and says, "Beware! Keep yourselves from covetousness, for a man's life doesn't consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses" (Luke 12:15), and then tells the story of a rich man who builds bigger barns for all his stuff and dies that same night (Luke 12:16–21). More of it

Once you start noticing

  • Straining gnats, swallowing camels. "You blind guides, who strain out a gnat, and swallow a camel!" (Matthew 23:24). Both animals were unclean under the law. Picture someone carefully filtering a bug out of their wine, then gulping down an entire camel, hump and all. Scholars think it may even be an Aramaic pun, since the words for gnat and camel sound alike.
  • The camel and the needle. "It is easier for a camel to go through a needle's eye" (Mark 10:25). Pure hyperbole, the biggest animal around and the smallest opening.
  • A lamp under the bed. "Is the lamp brought to be put under a basket or under a bed?" (Mark 4:21). Who lights a lamp and shoves it under the bed?
  • Nicknames. He calls the impulsive, wavering Simon "Rock" (Matthew 16:18), and two brothers with tempers "Sons of Thunder" (Mark 3:17).
  • The small-town burn. When Philip says they've found the Messiah and he's from Nazareth, Nathanael answers, "Can any good thing come out of Nazareth?" Jesus' first words to him: "Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom is no deceit!" (John 1:46–47). Basically, at least this guy's honest.
  • "Let the dead bury their own dead" (Matthew 8:22), said right after "the foxes have holes" to a follower who wants to wait. (More on that one.)
Why it matters

Holy people laugh

Spend time with the mystics and you'll notice they're often hilarious. Ramakrishna teased his disciples constantly. Neem Karoli Baba played tricks and cracked jokes under a blanket. The Dalai Lama giggles through press conferences. Mystics are sassy.

That's not an accident. Humor and awakening are close cousins. Both see through the ego's self-importance in a flash. When you see the beam in your own eye clearly enough, the right response isn't shame. It's a laugh. And then you put the beam down.
